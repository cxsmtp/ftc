# Hack Arena — Executive Summary

> **What it is:** A competitive engagement platform that turns product capabilities into hands-on challenges — for customers, prospects, partners, and developer communities.
> **Audience:** Sales leadership, marketing, and C-level sponsors

---

## The Business Case

Traditional demos and webinars tell your audience what your platform can do. Hack Arena makes them *experience* it — under competitive pressure, with a live audience, and a leaderboard keeping score.

The result is qualitatively different engagement: participants who have solved real problems using your product arrive at the next conversation with genuine understanding, internal credibility, and a personal stake in the outcome.

---

## What You Can Run

The platform is not one format. It runs three, and the choice shapes everything the participant meets — how they sign up, whether there is a leaderboard at all, and what they walk away with.

| | Shape | For | What they leave with |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Booth Event** | A few hours, one room, one session. Phone-solvable challenges, walk-up sign-up. Ranked | Conference stands, roadshows, an unplanned crowd | A ranked placing, a badge and a certificate — and a conversation |
| **Competition** | Online or hybrid, hours to days, one session or many. Ranked | Customer and partner hackathons, the flagship format | A ranked placing, a shareable badge, a certificate |
| **Program** | Self-paced, running to a deadline. No sessions, no leaderboard, no placings | Enablement and certification | A completion certificate, earned only on solving everything, that a third party can verify |

**A Program is a second business case, not a variation on the first.** It is the platform without the competition: no ranking, no podium, no urgency — just a curriculum with a deadline and a credential at the end. Its certificate is all or nothing: it is earned by solving every challenge in the set, and partial completion earns nothing, which is what makes it worth something to whoever asks to see it. That makes the same investment serve enablement, partner certification, and onboarding, not only campaigns.

Checkmarx One account provisioning is per-event and optional on all three — a Booth Event and a Program default to off, a Competition to on.

Everything below says which formats it applies to where it matters.

## 1. Awareness — Reaching New Audiences

A well-run event reaches audiences that would never attend a webinar. The competitive format travels. People share their rank. Colleagues sign up to beat each other. Partners invite their own networks.

- **Walk-up enrolment** — every event can publish a private join link, a QR code, and a **printed six-character code** for signage, so a conference booth or a room of guests self-registers in seconds without an administrator touching a keyboard. A Booth Event ships with this on; the other formats enable it when they want it
- **The shortest sign-up form the platform will produce** — what a walk-up is asked for is configured per event, field by field, and every event ships with the shortest default: five things typed (first and last name, work email, company, job title), with nickname, country and timezone filling themselves in and city and job function optional. A certification Program can ask for everything it needs. Fewer questions at a stand is directly fewer people who wander off
- **A work email is always required** — every registration needs one corporate address, on every format, and that is deliberate: it is what stops a join link circulated beyond the room being useful to strangers. Worth knowing before a stand, because a visitor offering only a personal address will be asked for a work one
- **Entry by emailed code** — participants never need a password to forget or leak: by default they sign in with a one-time code emailed to them, and an address that already has an account simply signs in to it. Administrators sign in with a password by default, anyone may choose either way for themselves, and a code can always be asked for instead, so a forgotten password is never a dead end. If mail is not arriving at all, an organiser reads a code straight off their own admin screen at the desk, with no email round trip — so a corporate mail filter stops being the reason somebody misses the event
- **Shareable achievement badges** — participants publish a branded badge to their LinkedIn feed and download a certificate for their training record. Ranked events award placement badges to the top three, when they scored above zero, and participation badges to everyone else who took part; a Program awards a completion certificate. **Nothing is published until the participant presses Share** — the platform hands off to LinkedIn's own composer and never posts on anyone's behalf, which is both the right default and a better story
- **Independently verifiable credentials** — each certificate carries a verification code, printed on the certificate itself beneath the address to check it at, and shown in the platform beside the holder's certificate. An employer or a customer types that code and the holder's surname into a public page and gets an authoritative answer, with no account and no JavaScript required. A certificate a third party can check is worth more than one that cannot, and it carries your platform's name into that check. Certificates issued before this capability existed carry no code and cannot be verified this way
- **Branded experience** — every participant touchpoint, including outbound mail, carries your platform's name, not a vendor's
- **Challenge design as storytelling** — each problem is an opportunity to demonstrate a product capability in a context that is immediately meaningful: participants learn what your platform does by using it to win

## 2. Engagement — Keeping Audiences Absorbed

Hack Arena is engineered so participants lean *in*, not just log on.

### Competitive mechanics that create emotional investment

- **Live leaderboard** *(Booth Events and Competitions)* — rankings update continuously with animated transitions; participants watch themselves rise or fall in real time, which drives repeat submissions and encourages help-seeking that surfaces product features
- **Gold / silver / bronze podium** *(Booth Events and Competitions)* — top three positions become aspirational targets for everyone watching; the social pressure is visible and self-reinforcing
- **Countdown urgency** — the timer turns red near the deadline; participants physically feel the pressure, which deepens the memory of the experience
- **Strategic depth** — hints are available at a point penalty; the risk/reward decision pulls participants deeper into the challenge rather than letting them give up and leave
- **Attempt limits, where you want them** — an event or an individual challenge can cap how many tries each participant gets, turning a guessing exercise into a considered one. An organiser can grant more on the spot to somebody who deserves another go
- **Instant feedback** — every submission returns an immediate result and an updated score, so the gratification loop keeps participants in flow

### Challenge design that showcases capabilities

- **Three difficulty tiers** — Easy through Hard with escalating point values; accessible entry point with a ceiling worth chasing, ensuring participants encounter progressively deeper product capabilities
- **Five question formats** — free-text answers (with any number of accepted spellings), structured data, uploaded files, multiple choice, and a free-response format that accepts whatever the participant writes and hands the organiser every response in the export — an open question, a reflection, or a use case captured inside the event; the format follows the capability being demonstrated rather than forcing every problem into one shape. A Booth Event deliberately refuses file uploads and anything needing a desktop tool, because its challenges have to be solvable on a phone in the room
- **Categories** — challenges can be grouped into named tracks that participants see on each challenge and can search by, so a mixed bank reads as a curriculum rather than a list
- **Rich content** — formatted descriptions, embedded images, tables, code samples, and downloadable reference material mean challenges can mirror real-world scenarios, not toy problems
- **Staged release** — new challenges can be unlocked mid-event, building surprise, extending engagement, and giving participants a reason to keep checking back
- **Author recognition** — each challenge can credit the person who wrote it, giving internal experts public visibility and incentivising quality challenge authorship

## 3. Loyalty — Building a Returning Community

A single event is a campaign. A recurring one is a community.

Hack Arena is built for return visits. The platform preserves participant identity, history, and reputation across events, which transforms one-time attendees into invested community members.

- **Historical continuity** — participant profiles accumulate across events; the platform remembers who they are, what they solved, and how they performed, and an organiser can open any participant's full history from one screen
- **Reusable cohorts** — participants can be organised into named groups that persist between events and bind to new ones in a single step, with membership kept in sync both ways. Last quarter's partner cohort becomes this quarter's invitation list
- **Team competition** *(ranked formats, on request)* — a group can also compete as a team, with a team leaderboard ranked on average performance alongside the individual one. Team play converts a solo exercise into a shared one, which is what turns colleagues into repeat attendees — and the standings are frozen the moment an event ends, so a result published to a customer stays true
- **Challenge reuse without spoilers** — answers stay hidden until every session of an event has closed, and only if the organiser chooses to reveal them at all, so the same challenge set can be reused across recurring events without compromising integrity for later cohorts
- **Partner and ecosystem events** — invite ISV partners, customer champions, or community members to a shared competitive experience that is qualitatively stronger than any briefing or demo
- **Delegated organisers** — a partner or a business unit can be granted organising powers over *specific* events and nothing outside them. That is what makes a shared programme practical: they run their event, see their participants, and cannot reach anybody else's. Creating events, platform settings and the request logs stay with your own administrators
- **Late arrivals still count** — a Booth Event's door can stay open after the stand closes, so somebody who arrives at the end — scanning the event's code, or added by an organiser at the desk — still takes part and still earns a certificate, which is marked as a late entry and ranked on its own board so it takes nothing from the people who were there on time
- **Internal reference scores** — trusted experts can be flagged as benchmarks; their results are shown separately to organisers as a yardstick for how hard a challenge set actually proved, without appearing on the participants' board
- **Staff can compete, where you choose** — organisers and helpers stay off every board by default, and a single switch on an event lets the staff enrolled in it compete like anybody else, on the board and eligible for a certificate. The moment they are enrolled they can no longer change that event or any challenge in it, whatever their tier, and the event may hold only sealed challenges — so a colleague competing against the room cannot read the answer key

## 4. Intelligence — Turning Engagement into Action

Every action a participant takes is a signal. Hack Arena structures those signals so your team can act on them immediately after the event.

### What the platform captures automatically

- **Problem-solving capability** — who solved which challenge, how quickly, at what difficulty level
- **Knowledge gaps** — hint usage reveals where participants needed help, pointing directly to education opportunities and product-fit conversations
- **Persistence signals** — wrong-answer frequency and retry behaviour distinguish participants who are genuinely invested from those who are just present
- **Engagement time** *(Competitions and Programs)* — optionally, how much time each participant actually spent on the platform, as distinct from how long the event ran. On a multi-day event that is the difference between a number that ranks nobody and one that tells you something. Read it as a signal of engagement rather than a measure of effort: it counts activity in the participant's own browser, so work done in an IDE or a terminal does not appear
- **Satisfaction data** — participants rate each challenge from 1 to 5; real-time content-quality feedback that also doubles as an engagement depth signal
- **Free-response answers** — a challenge can ask an open question instead of checking an answer, and every participant's response is captured exactly as written and exported beside their profile, so a reflection, a piece of feedback or a described use case collected mid-event lands in the same file as the roster
- **Professional context** — company and title are captured at every registration; the rest of the profile is configurable per event, so what you collect at a booth and what you collect for a certification can differ

### What you look at

- **An analytics dashboard** — solve rates, unique attempters, and the incorrect / hint / abandoned breakdown per challenge, filtered by event, time window, difficulty, track, or cohort. This is how you learn which challenges worked and which need rewriting before the next run
- **Exports** — the participant roster with full professional context, as CSV or JSON, and — exported from within an event — each participant's free-response answers beside their profile; the rankings as CSV, with a per-participant breakdown of points lost to wrong answers and hints; a per-participant × per-challenge activity file for the whole event; one ZIP with everything about an event; and a person's complete history
- **Follow-up sent for you** — when an event ends, every eligible participant is emailed their certificate with a ready-to-post LinkedIn message, and the event's own team gets its report: each sales development contact their hot leads with a suggested opener and a file of them, marketing the full lead file, managers a scorecard against the event's targets, with a reminder 48 hours later about hot leads nobody has contacted

### From event to pipeline

1. Export the participant roster → load into Salesforce, HubSpot, or Dynamics
2. Tier participants by leaderboard rank, challenge completion, and company
3. Flag top performers for high-touch follow-up or community leadership invitations
4. Use hint and rating data to personalise product conversations around demonstrated knowledge gaps

### Global reach, fair comparison

- **Multi-timezone sessions** *(Competitions)* — the same event runs across Asia, EMEA, and the Americas in separate time windows; participants compete within their own cohort with independent rankings by default
- **Events in parallel** — run as many campaigns at once as you like, across product lines, business units, or geographies, each with its own audience, organisers, and leaderboard
- **Bulk enrolment** — up to a thousand participants assigned per operation, with identity provisioning into connected systems handled automatically where an event is configured for it

## Assurance

The two questions this audience asks next.

- **Access control and accountability** — four built-in roles plus custom roles built from a permission list. For the scoped organiser role every administrative route is refused by default until a human has classified it, so a route added next year by somebody who has never heard of that role fails closed; every route is classified, and the build fails if a new one is missing. Administrative and participant actions that change state are recorded with the acting identity and the source IP, held to that standard by automated checks that fail the build when a new route emits an unattributed event; scheduled and background work is attributed to the system
- **Retention** — the structured audit history is kept for a configurable window, two years by default, and purged automatically by a daily sweep. Request logs are rotated daily and reclaimed under disk pressure, with a retention script for operators who need a time-based log policy
- **Session and abuse controls** — one session per user, and removing an administrator's access stops even an upload or import they already had under way; ten wrong sign-in codes or passwords in five minutes emails the account holder, while each sign-in door's rate limits bound the guessing itself; the correct code or password still works and clears the alarm, so it cannot be turned against a participant mid-event; rate limiting throughout; and automated blocking of scanning traffic. Every block is logged and audited, and administrators are alerted whenever the traffic could be legitimate — a shared venue network, say — while unmistakable scanner activity is blocked silently, so the alerts stay worth reading
- **No third-party code in the browser** — fonts, charting, syntax highlighting and QR generation are all served from your own deployment; the content-security policy names no external origin and Trusted Types are enforced. The only outbound destination is one the user chooses: sharing to their own LinkedIn feed
- **Verified continuously** — several thousand automated tests run on every change, including accessibility checks and pixel-level visual regression, with a separate cross-browser pass over the paths that must work on every engine
- **Measured under load** — four-hour runs at 300 concurrent participants, against a database sized like a mature deployment and over an emulated wide-area link with real latency, jitter and loss. The current baseline was taken on an earlier build and is due to be re-run
